WHAT WE COVERED
=================
This was a six-claim cold-quiz built as a peer-review scenario: a design doc
from another team's FGCP cluster build, with six specific claims to mark
correct or wrong. All six claims were deliberately built from Cluster A1 of
the initial weakness register (Sessions 16 and 17), the highest exam-frequency
cluster identified in the baseline.

Claim 1 tested the heartbeat EtherType, isolating why 0x8100 (802.1Q VLAN
tagging) is an unrelated field that only superficially resembles 0x8890
(FGCP hello) as a hex string. The student initially reached for VLAN
tagging as the mechanism, then reasoned correctly to "the frame needs its
own identifying field" before being given the actual value.

Claim 2 tested hb-lost-threshold, deliberately baiting the common "3 missed
heartbeats" convention borrowed from other protocols (OSPF-style thinking).
The student initially agreed with the bait, then reversed after being asked
to weigh 600ms of tolerance against real-world noise (CPU spikes, STP
recalculation) and correctly concluded the claim was "trigger happy."

Claim 3 tested whether dual heartbeat links load-balance. The student
correctly rejected load-balancing in favor of ordered/exclusive behavior
(lower hbdev priority number wins, backup sits idle) and correctly
identified that session-sync-dev — not heartbeat — is the one that
actually load-balances.

Claim 4 tested override-off semantics with the classic short-outage vs
long-outage trap. The student correctly reasoned that a sub-5-minute
outage produces an uptime tie, which falls through to priority — meaning
override-off does NOT guarantee "no preemption ever."

Claim 5 tested the A-A load-balancing hash input, correctly ruling out IP
Identification (varies per packet, fails the same-session-same-box test)
in favor of the 4-tuple.

Claim 6 tested the A-A return path, correctly identifying that Box B
replies directly using its own physical MAC rather than relaying back
through Box A — the single highest-value fact in the cluster, since it
explains why single-box packet captures on the primary can look like a
failed handshake for a session that completed successfully.
