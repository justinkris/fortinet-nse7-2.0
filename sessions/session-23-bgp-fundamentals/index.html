Concepts Learned

1AD vs best-path scope: Administrative Distance (Static 10, eBGP 20, OSPF 110, iBGP 200) resolves conflicts BETWEEN protocols. It is silent when two routes of the SAME protocol (e.g., two eBGP peers) compete — that gap is filled by BGP's own ordered best-path attribute list, because unlike OSPF's shared bandwidth-derived cost formula, no shared formula exists between two independent ISPs.

2. Full best-path order (11 steps): Weight (router-local only, default 0/32768) → Local Preference (iBGP-only, stripped at eBGP boundary, default 100) → locally-originated → AS-Path length (shortest; NOT a performance metric, just an AS-boundary count) → Origin type (IGP < EGP < Incomplete) → MED (lowest, compared per-neighbor-AS by default) → eBGP over iBGP → lowest IGP metric to next-hop → oldest route → lowest Router ID → lowest Neighbor IP. BGP stops at the first attribute that differs; nothing lower is ever consulted.

3. The outbound/inbound steering distinction (the session's central insight): Weight and Local Preference steer YOUR OWN outbound traffic — guaranteed, because the comparison happens entirely on your own router, against routes you already received. AS-Path prepending and MED only influence INBOUND traffic by shaping what you advertise outward — never guaranteed, because the neighboring AS runs its own best-path process on its own hardware. AS-Path prepending works universally (every AS must count hops); MED only works if the neighboring AS's engineer configured policy to honor it — MED is a request, not a law.

4. ASN semantics: an ASN identifies one routing domain/policy, not a device — hence FG-EDGE-P and FG-EDGE-DR correctly share one ASN (same company, same policy) while ISP-A/ISP-B carry unrelated ASNs. Unlike an OSPF Area ID (locally meaningful, freely reusable), an ASN must be globally unique (IANA/RIR-registered) because BGP's loop-prevention mechanism checks every received AS-Path for the router's own ASN and silently drops any match — duplicate ASNs break even non-overlapping, unrelated traffic the moment a route transits near the collision.

5. eBGP's TTL=1 default: BGP rides TCP; FortiGate's default eBGP TTL is 1, assuming a direct physical cross-connect to the neighbor. Peers genuinely more than one hop away produce total silence (TCP SYN discarded en route, no error, no neighbor ever appears) — same failure family as S22's OSPF hello-gate mismatches. `ebgp-multihop` is the FortiOS knob that relaxes this for legitimate non-adjacent eBGP scenarios; introduced conceptually only, full CLI deferred to S24.

Important Takeaways

  • AD resolves cross-protocol conflicts; BGP's own ordered attribute list resolves same-protocol (eBGP-vs-eBGP) conflicts. Different jobs, different tools.
  • The best-path ladder is walked top-down and stops at the first differing attribute — a favorable value further down the list never rescues a route that already lost above it.
  • Weight and Local Preference steer YOUR outbound traffic, guaranteed (your hardware, your decision). AS-Path prepending and MED only request influence over INBOUND traffic, never guaranteed (their hardware, their decision).
  • AS-Path length is a policy proxy (AS boundaries crossed), not a performance metric.
  • ASNs must be globally unique because BGP's loop-prevention check scans every AS-Path for your own ASN — duplication breaks unrelated traffic, not just overlapping prefixes.
  • eBGP defaults to TTL=1, assuming direct adjacency; non-adjacent legitimate peering needs `ebgp-multihop`, or the neighbor silently never appears.

Audio Podcast Prompt (For "The Packet Path")

Write a two-host podcast script for "The Packet Path," a storytelling podcast for network engineers. Host: Alex, an experienced network security consultant. Co-host: Sam, smart and curious, asks clarifying questions. Tone: intelligent, conversational, true-crime documentary style for network engineers. Episode title: "The Router That Asked Nicely." Length: 12–15 minutes of dialogue.

The case: a financial company finally dual-homes its primary datacenter to two internet providers, ISP-A and ISP-B, closing the single-point-of-failure gap that caused a 55-minute outage months earlier. But now both providers hand back a route to the same destination — and the network's own protocol has no shared formula to judge them by, because they're two completely separate companies with nothing in common except the customer between them.

Beats, in order: (1) The setup — Administrative Distance, the trusted ladder that's ranked every routing source for months, goes silent for the first time: both candidate routes are eBGP, same rank, nothing to say. (2) Alex reveals BGP doesn't have a cost formula at all — it has an ordered list of opinions, walked one at a time, because "which path is better" between two rival companies is a policy question no shared math can answer. (3) The private knob: Weight, so local it won't even tell your own sister router down the hall. Then Local Preference, the same opinion shared honestly with every router in your own house — but silent the instant it tries to cross into someone else's. (4) The twist Sam has to sit with: the router doesn't get to choose who reaches it. Traffic leaving your building, you control completely. Traffic arriving at your door is a decision made entirely on a stranger's hardware, using a stranger's math. (5) The blunt tool — AS-Path prepending, deliberately making your own advertisement look worse, hoping every network on earth's shared law about counting hops does the persuading for you. (6) The polite tool — MED, an actual number sent across the border, except this one isn't a law, it's a request, and the neighbor's engineer can simply decide not to read the letter. (7) The identity crisis — what an AS number really protects: not politeness, but a loop-detection tripwire; two companies sharing one number by mistake doesn't just create administrative confusion, it silently kills real traffic that happens to pass near the collision, for no reason either company can see in their own logs. (8) The silent neighbor — TTL set to 1, assuming your ISP's router is standing right next to yours; break that assumption and you don't get an error, you get nothing at all, forever. Close with Alex's line: "BGP doesn't get to give orders outside its own house. Everything past your own front door is a request — and the internet is under no obligation to say yes."

Sam should ask: "Wait, so my own router can't decide who reaches me?", "If MED is just a request, why does anyone bother sending it?", "Two companies use the same AS number by accident — how does that break traffic that isn't even theirs?", and "The neighbor never showed up and there's no error — how do you even start troubleshooting that?"
